AI Architecture and Governance Readiness

A readiness assessment from the Responsible AI and adaptive systems area.

Examining how far the organization has the information foundations, ownership, controls and monitoring needed to adopt AI responsibly.

The area applies architecture, information governance and sociotechnical analysis to the responsible adoption of systems that learn, adapt or automate consequential decisions. The questions are architectural before they are technical: what the system depends on, who owns it, how its behavior is monitored, and where human judgment stays in the loop.

The assessment considers data lineage, system accountability, human oversight, model monitoring and the relationship between adaptive systems and existing architecture governance.

This assessment is carried out through the AI Governance and Information Readiness QuickScan, a fixed-fee engagement.

The QuickScan

Four questions to ask before extending AI use

AI arrives in an organization in more ways than one: a dedicated initiative, a copilot, or a feature switched on by default in a supplier's existing product. However it arrives, the same four questions follow:

  • Who is accountable when one of these systems gets it wrong?
  • What evidence supports an output, and what are the limits of explaining it?
  • Is the data underneath it actually fit for the decision it is informing?
  • What happens when the supplier changes the model?

These are not only technical questions. They reach into ownership, information and architecture, and answering them means gathering evidence from across boundaries that no single team owns.

The QuickScan establishes how far these questions can be answered today, where the evidence is missing, and which decisions follow. It works through an agreed application as a practical lens: what that system reveals about ownership, data, architecture and control can point to gaps beyond it.

The report gives leadership, management and architecture a shared basis for discussing the findings, the limits of the available evidence, and the decisions that need attention.

The framework already exists

The QuickScan applies a defined assessment framework: five dimensions, a structured line of questioning, and a fixed reporting format. The framework is informed by doctoral research into complex sociotechnical systems — where outcomes emerge from interactions between parts rather than from any single component — and by architecture practice in regulated environments.

Because the method is defined, it provides a consistent structure for gathering evidence, examining gaps and reporting findings. It also makes a fixed fee possible for an agreed scope.

Scope and schedule are both agreed at intake and recorded in the engagement letter: which application serves as the lens, who is interviewed, what the report covers, and when each step happens. The schedule depends on access to documentation and to the people who own the system, so it is set together rather than assumed.

Who commissions it

Built for medium-sized public and semi-public organizations that are using, piloting or preparing to introduce AI, and do not yet have coherent governance or a roadmap. The question usually sits with CIOs, CDOs, corporate and enterprise architects, information managers, and program managers for AI or digital transformation.

What is examined

The agreed application is examined from five connected angles, and each one asks a question about the organization rather than about the system:

  • Purpose and public value — which problem is being solved, and how will success be judged?
  • Governance and accountability — who decides, who oversees, and where is human intervention required?
  • Information and data — whether a customer, a case or an asset means the same thing across the systems that exchange it, which source is authoritative, and whether meaning, quality, provenance, access and retention are sufficiently controlled. Data can be complete and technically sound and still produce two irreconcilable answers.
  • Architecture and integration — how does the application fit the existing application, information and security landscape?
  • Risk and control — what arises around privacy, security, transparency, supplier dependency and applicable obligations?

The QuickScan is an architecture and governance assessment. It is not a legal audit and not a formal certification.

What it produces

  • Readiness findings across the five dimensions, with strengths and the most significant gaps.
  • A distinction between application-specific observations, wider organizational findings supported by evidence, and issues requiring further investigation.
  • The risks, dependencies and decisions that need attention, and the places where the evidence to answer them does not yet exist.
  • Five prioritized measures for the next ninety days.
  • A report and a closing session in which the findings are presented and tested.

How it runs

An intake to agree which application will serve as the lens and to fix the scope and schedule, focused document analysis, interviews with the people who own and operate it or who are designing it, and a closing session with leadership.

The lens is not chosen at random. The most useful starting point is usually the place where inconsistency is already causing visible friction — the integration that needs constant manual reconciliation, the report nobody fully trusts, the system two departments describe differently. Friction that is already felt makes the findings immediately demonstrable.

Where the lens is a proposed rather than an operating application, the findings concern documented plans and the preconditions that would have to be met. Controls that are not yet running are not described as verified.

The organization keeps one point of contact available throughout and supplies the agreed documentation and interview access on the dates set at intake.

What it costs

The QuickScan is delivered for a fixed fee, agreed before the work starts. Scope is set at intake, and the fee remains fixed for the agreed scope.

Applications beyond the agreed scope, a target architecture, a transformation roadmap, deeper legal assessment, technical implementation and support after delivery fall outside the scan and can be agreed separately.

The result

The organization receives an evidence-based view of readiness within the agreed scope, with wider organizational implications identified where the evidence supports them. The findings support decisions about the application examined, the preconditions requiring attention, and the responsibilities that need clarification.

Where the findings call for a target architecture or a transformation roadmap, that work can be agreed as a separate engagement.
